## Post 29 (Unknown date)

Winter Hoof Growth

Here in Massachusetts, winter came earlier than usual this year. Inevitably with it come the questions about winter hoofcare cycles - 
“how long do you think they can go between trims now that their growth has slowed?” 

“They don’t really need another trim in 5 weeks do they?”

Now I’ll try to make this short and sweet. As a hoofcare provider, I am fighting for my life to get horses’ hooves in a better spot all spring, summer, and fall- often fighting against metabolic concerns, grass intake, excessive growth, distortion even on a regular schedule, etc. 

But for all the horsekeeping issues we deal with in winter, at least for many horses winter gives us a little reprieve in their excessive growth and potential distortion. This is the time we can get ahead of hoof issues- when we can address the flare before it starts, when we can work on correcting angles without doing too much all at once, when we can get heels to come back under the limb and get breakover in a good spot, and we can get the entire horse moving and feeling better. 

Although it’s tempting to want to stretch out a horse‘s hoofcare cycle in winter, and while for some that may be an okay option, I am most excited when l can stick to the same cycle year round and make even better progress all winter and set the feet up to be healthier when the spring growth hits. 

Our job as hoofcare providers isn’t just removing excess. It’s addressing hoof balance, supporting the feet for proper movement and comfort, and addressing issues before they become pathological or chronic. 

If your hoofcare provider is asking to stay on the same cycle this winter, trust that they’re trying to make sure the feet don’t get into a spot you have to rehab from next summer.

This series draws exclusively from peer-reviewed veterinary research, including studies from UC Davis, Cornell University, and official .gov and .edu sources—combined with firsthand clinical experience managing thousands of horses in Florida, including hundreds of critical care patients.

✍️ Written by Andrew Vanlangendonck
Director of Rehabilitation Services
Equine Performance and Innovative Center
Ocala, Florida

🔥☠️ Florida Heat & Horses ☠️🔥

Part 3: The Aftermath, the Myths, and the Mistakes That Still Kill

🧠⚠️ The Final Post in the Series ⚠️ 🧠

“I’d rather waste a minute of my life than lose a life in one minute.”

This post is that minute. Don’t waste it.

—

📍 What We’re Still Seeing
Despite record shares, reach, and reads—we’re still getting panicked calls for horses down, horses not sweating, horses dehydrated but refusing to drink.

The biggest killers?
❌ Misinformation
❌ Delayed action
❌ Trust in the wrong people

And now, the silent wave begins—the aftershocks from horses that “seemed fine” days ago.

—

🩺 The Aftermath: Days Later, Still at Risk
✅ They cooled off.
✅ They drank.
✅ They walked away… or so you thought.

📉 What we’re seeing 24–72 hours later:
• Kidney damage – dehydration, NSAIDs, electrolyte mismanagement
• Liver enzyme spikes – hyperthermia, dehydration, metabolic crash
• Colic – ileus, impaction, gut shutdown
• Laminitis – under-recognized heat trigger
• Anhidrosis onset – body gives up on sweating
• Appetite loss, dull coat, dark urine – internal stress

📚 Confirmed by Cornell, UC Davis, peer-reviewed equine heat stroke data (PMC10267279).
Even one overheating episode can leave permanent organ damage.

—

“He’s just quiet” = 🚨 until proven otherwise

💧 The Critical Dehydration Threshold 💧

Once a horse loses 6–8% of body water, they are clinically dehydrated.
By the time you notice, they’ve already lost liters of fluid.

📌 Once that happens, they often stop drinking.
Why?
• Thirst shuts down when sodium is imbalanced
• Gut absorption slows or stops
• The body begins internal shutdown

🧪 At this stage, IV fluids or NG tubing may be the only way to rehydrate.
⚠️ Don’t wait. If they’ve stopped drinking, you’re already behind.

—

🚫 The Fatal Fixes People Still Use 🚫

💥 Electrolyte tubes without water → worsens dehydration
💥 Electrolyte-laced grains → spike sugar, stress metabolic horses
💥 NSAIDs like Banamine, Bute, Equioxx → tank kidney perfusion
💥 Hose in the mouth → aspiration risk
💥 Hose in the rectum → gut rupture, electrolyte crash
💥 ACE → lowers blood pressure, shuts down organ perfusion

📌 Every one of these most likely killed a horse  this week.

—

🧪 The Myths That Can’t Seem To Die
🛑 “He’s sweating = safe” → NO. Sweating means water loss.
🛑 “Electrolytes fix it” → Not without water.
🛑 “He’s acclimated” → Even Florida-born horses are dropping.
🛑 “I rinsed and fanned him” → Needs continuous cold water + airflow.
🛑 “Alcohol burns” → 1:1 or 1:2 alcohol-to-water = safe, vet approved.
🛑 “Ice water caused colic” → Wrong. The colic was already happening.

—

🧊 True Cooling = Cold Water + Airflow + Repeat
• Cold hose (50–65°F)
• Don’t scrape
• Add diluted alcohol
• Cold towels to neck/groin
• Shade and airflow
• Repeat for 20–30 minutes minimum

✅ Backed by: UC Davis, Cornell, AAEP, Ohmura et al. 2023

—

🌡️ Why Florida Is More Lethal Than Texas
= Humidity.

🔥 111°F + 90% humidity = 176°F heat index
🌙 Nights drop to “96°F feels like 106°F”
No relief ~ no recovery
Sweat won’t evaporate ~ internal boil

🧠 Even shaded, “acclimated” horses are cooking from the inside out.

—

📌 BOTTOM LINE:
👉🏼 Not drinking, Not sweating, Not peeing, or Not acting right? 
👉🏼 ACT NOW!
👉🏼 Don’t trust myths or Facebook advice.

You get ONE chance during collapse. After that? Just damage control—if you’re lucky.

—

📣 SHARE THIS. Tape it to your feed room. Send it to your barn.

Because someone’s horse is about to go down.
And this might be the post that saves them.

—

🧪 1. Baseline Bloodwork for At-Risk Horses
Metabolic, senior, performance, and anhidrotic horses should get preemptive panels:
• BUN, Creatinine
• AST, GGT, Bilirubin
• Sodium, Potassium, Chloride, CO₂
• Glucose, Albumin, Total Protein, CK

📚 Cornell and UC Davis recommend early screening to catch silent issues before collapse.

—

🧂 2. Switch from Salt Blocks to Loose Electrolytes
Salt blocks are useless in summer.
A horse can’t lick fast enough to replace 30–50g sodium lost/hour in sweat.

✅ Use plain salt + balanced electrolyte powders in every meal.
🧠 Critical for anhidrosis-prone, hard-working, or pastured horses in heat.

—

💨 3. Build a Swamp Cooling Stall
• Overhead and box fans
• Wet stall walls and bedding
• Ice water towels + misting alcohol
✅ Field-tested in Ocala: drops temp faster than hosing alone

—

📸 4. Use Thermal Imaging
Smartphone FLIR cameras detect:
• Poor sweat zones
• Limb inflammation
• Internal hotspots
✅ Catch heat injuries before clinical signs

—

🧠 5. Train Your Staff
Laminate posters:
• TPR cheat sheet
• 20+ min cooling protocol
• When to call vet
• What NOT to give
✅ Training = lives saved

—

🩸 6. Monitor the Recovery Phase (24–72 hrs)
Even if they “look better,” internal damage progresses:
• Creatinine and BUN spike
• Liver enzymes rise
• Gut perfusion tanks
• CK surges → rhabdo
• Neurologic signs days later

✅ Twice daily vitals, proactive fluids, repeat bloodwork

—

💊 7. Avoid DIY “Hydration” Mashes
• High molasses = more heat
• Poor electrolyte balance
• Fiber with no absorption unless soaked

✅ Stick to soaked hay, plain beet pulp, balanced electrolytes

—

🚫 8. NEVER Force Water by Hose or Rectum
Risks:
• Choke
• Aspiration
• Rectal rupture
• Gut shutdown
✅ No drinking means call a vet for IV or NG fluids

—

🧪 9. Horses That Stop Drinking = Red Alert
• Thirst mechanism fails
• Gut shuts down
• Electrolyte crash
✅ IV fluids may reset thirst in hours. DO NOT WAIT.

—

🧂 10. Only Use Electrolyte Paste if Gut Is Working
Signs NOT to paste:
• Dry gums
• No gut sounds
• No urine

✅ If eating, drinking, pooping: paste is okay.
Otherwise: fluids first.

—

🧠 11. Heat Stroke Can Trigger Permanent Anhidrosis
Damage to sweat glands, receptors, and thermoregulation may be irreversible.
📚 UF study: One collapse = higher chance of lifelong sweat loss.
✅ Monitor for sweat output after every heat event.

—

🏇 12. Performance Loss Can Last Weeks
Even “recovered” horses show:
• Reduced VO₂ max
• Poor muscle recovery
• Impaired hydration regulation
📚 Cornell & JEVS studies: 2–4 weeks for full return to baseline

—

📈 13. Track the Trends
Signs before collapse:
• Loose manure
• Corner-standing
• Subtle stiffness
• Dark pee
• Low water intake

✅ Daily logs catch issues early

—

🧪 14. Repeat Bloodwork at 48–72 hrs
📚 AAEP: Delayed organ failure is common post-heat.
✅ Watch Creatinine, BUN, GGT, AST, CK, Albumin, Glucose

—

📖 15. Create a Heat Episode Logbook
Include:
• Vitals at collapse
• Cooling timeline
• Fluids used
• Products tolerated
• Vet notes
• Triggers

✅ This log saves lives in future heat events.

—

🔥 16. Subclinical Heat Injury Exists
📚 J Vet Internal Med: Horses with no collapse still developed:
• Muscle soreness
• Dehydration
• Organ stress
• Mood changes

✅ Treat every high-risk day seriously—even if “nothing happened”

—

⚠️ 17. Salt Alone Can Be Dangerous
Salt ≠ electrolytes.
Horses sweat out potassium, calcium, magnesium—not just sodium.

📚 Cornell: Replacing only sodium = worsens dehydration and thirst shutdown

✅ Complete formulas only

—

🩻 18. Post-Heat Laminitis Risk
Heat + gut shutdown + glucose spike = lamellar damage
📚 UC Davis + AAEP: Rads 3–5 days post-collapse in metabolic horses
✅ Catch it before mechanical failure

—

🧠 19. Heat Stroke = Higher Anhidrosis Risk Later
📚 UF study: Even fully recovered horses may stop sweating months later.
✅ Log TPR, sweat, and behavior every heat wave

—

💩 20. Leaky Gut After Heat = Weeks of Inflammation
Core temps >103°F damage gut lining
📚 Cornell + BMC: LPS leaks → colic, ulcers, immune crash

✅ Support gut with pectin-lecithin, S. boulardii, soaked hay

—

⚡ 21. Behavior Changes = Physical Damage
Post-heat:
• Spooky
• Grumpy
• Stopping at jumps
• Resistant to girth

✅ Full exam. Don’t assume it’s “just behavior”

—

📅 22. Acclimation Wears Off
📚 Comparative Exercise Physiology: Acclimation lasts 2–3 weeks
Rain, night turnout, or time off resets the clock

✅ Monitor ALL horses—even Florida natives

—

🧬 23. Subclinical Kidney Damage = Silent Killer
• High-normal Creatinine
• Persistent BUN
📚 SDMA, urine protein:creatinine ratios = better markers

✅ Avoid NSAIDs for 72 hrs post-event. Rehydrate fully.

—

🔁 24. Cyclic Heat Spikes Are Worse Than Consistent Heat
📚 NCSU + Texas A&M: Repeated spikes wreck thermoregulation
✅ Hydration/rest based on trends—not just the daily high

—

💉 25. Meds Stay in System = Delayed Collapse
• ACE → impairs thermoregulation
• Bute → hides symptoms, damages kidneys
• Gabapentin → dulls heat distress response

✅ Avoid or reduce workload 48–72 hrs post-medication

—

🧪 26. Glucose & Liver Values Predict Recovery Time
📚 UC Davis:
• High glucose = stress response
• High GGT/AST = gut-liver inflammation
✅ Soaked forage, Vitamin E, liver support for 7–10 days post-event

—

🦠 27. Heat = Mold & Mycotoxin Spike
• Aspergillus, Fusarium, Penicillium
📚 Cornell: Symptoms mimic heat stroke

✅ Store hay dry, discard soaked feed, avoid sweet feeds

—

🧫 28. Endotoxin Rebound = Delayed Death
Even after cooling, gut sloughs → LPS leak → laminitis, lethargy, fever
📚 UC Davis + CSU
✅ Sucralfate, Biosponge, omega-3s, track vitals 48 hrs minimum

—

🔥 29. Internal Hyperthermia Lasts Hours
Rectal temp may read 101.5°F… but deep tissue still cooking
✅ Continue cooling long after normal readings
✅ Watch behavior, urination, manure

—

🥩 30. High-Protein Feeds Raise Internal Heat

14% CP = metabolic heat load
📚 Cornell + KER: Avoid alfalfa, high-protein grain post-collapse

✅ Use soaked hay + fat-based feeds only

—

🧂 31. Salt Craving ≠ Thirst
Licking people, dirt, buckets = sodium/zinc deficiency
📚 KER: Water and salt must both be replaced

✅ Salt in feed, soaked mash, and electrolyte water

—

🩸 32. Hemoconcentration Fools Bloodwork
“Normal” PCV + TP can hide dehydration
✅ Skin tent and MM moisture = more reliable in early crisis

—

🧠 33. Brain Damage From Heat Stroke Is Real
• Ataxia
• Circling
• Aggression
• Seizures (especially minis, foals)

📚 J Vet Internal Med: MRI-confirmed hippocampal damage in one survivor
✅ Monitor neuro signs, use Vitamin E, consider imaging

—

💪 34. Rhabdo Can Shut Down Kidneys Days Later
CK spike → myoglobin in blood → clogs renal tubules
✅ Watch urine color, flank pain, hydration status

—

🧯 35. Geriatrics & Minis Crash Quietly
Slow decline:
• Less drinking
• Dry manure
• Subtle mood change

📚 AAEP + Cornell: “Atypical collapse” = just as fatal
✅ Intervene early, hydrate, monitor TPR daily

## Post 54 (Unknown date)

What Are You Looking At?

COR or CORE?

The left pic shows the result of farrier forced COR principles on the front hoof of this 22 yr old
Grand Prix level Warm  blood mare.  The red area is of the most concern to me, as this is where arthritic changes appear. In this pic, there are signs of strain, atrophy, and collapse. The green area is the living supple hoof capsule that can be used to assess progress as it grows down. The blue area is the retained dead hoof capsule that should grow all the way out for a full rehab. This “old foot/new foot” stage occurs when the hoof capsule is allowed to grow more than it’s allowed to wear, or be properly trimmed to simulate wear. 

The right pic shows the same hoof 18 months later, after corrective and rehabilitative trimming to restore this horse’s true angles. You can see that the hoof is fully live, the retained dead hoof capsule has been grown off,  and growth/wear equilibrium has been restored. There is no blue area. The red area has been “pumped up”, showing evidence of significant healing. 

I believe in the importance of the COR principles. I just don’t believe in propping the horse up on false heel height, steel shoes, wedges and DIM to get there prematurely.  If there was an accompanying x-ray with the pic on the left, you would see that although there is a slightly straighter HPA, the spacing of the lower 3 joints may not be aligned, and the navicular bone may also be out of alignment and jammed up into the coffin joint. This is where the “crumpled” effect in the foot on the left comes from. There is not enough soft tissue and live sole depth for a proper elastic foundation. My focus goes less to the angles and more to the health and strength of the bare hoof. I believe that should be optimized before considering a prosthetic, unless the prosthetic is actually going to improve the health and strength of the foot, rather than just keep the horse going for a couple more years. The problem is that most people are looking at X-rays that only show things like boney alignment and sole depth. People are hyper focused on HPA and PA instead of soft tissue, live sole depth, and joint spacing. There is plenty of, if not more, valuable information on the outside of the hoof. You just need to read the obvious writing on the wall and trust your gut more. 

Frequent proper trimming to simulate natural wear and promote healthy development works by knowing the shapes of the internal anatomy and finding the natural shapes in the external anatomy to achieve the true hoof capsule/P3 alignment.  There are more curves than straight lines, flat planes, and angles inside and out. These curves need to be respected and built over time to achieve optimal alignment and integrity. The shaping of the outside of the foot influences the development of the inside of the foot, which again goes back to influencing the outside of the foot. 

Imo, The COR principles should only be used to assess progress, not as a forced ideal.  True strength comes from building the “ core “ of the digital cushion ( collecting the foot ), not from flattening and propping a weak foot up on a false external foundation. 

*Maronda has an album on Hoof Builders. She has been in training for the entire rehab process and has shown at least 3 times during these 18 months. A full rehab typically takes me 24 months. Fully live well developed feet typically take me 4 years. I can see visible improvement in the feet for up to 6 years. I can see improvements in movement beyond that. Age and time in shoes or improperly trimmed feet are also factors, so I always recommend starting to build the feet as early as possible and if you’re going to use shoes, avoid  flat trimming, metal, and wedges.

## Post 66 (Unknown date)

Your horse NEEDS at least 1-2tbsp of salt per day. 

Plain white salt. 

Fancy pretty salts aren’t necessary and for the love-please remove the red mineral block. 

Chances are they aren’t getting their minimum amount from a block-but a white block is great to offer should they need to self serve. 

Horses need salt (sodium chloride) for essential bodily functions like maintaining fluid balance, muscle and nerve function, and encouraging drinking, which is crucial for hydration. Salt is not produced by the body and must be provided in the diet. 

Ensure fresh, clean water is always available, as excess salt can lead to dehydration if water intake is insufficient. 

Horses in heavy work or warm climates may require more-https://madbarn.com/how-to-feed-salt-to-your-horse/
